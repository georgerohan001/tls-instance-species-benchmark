Abstract

Individual-tree inventories remain labour-intensive, while deep learning on terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) promises scalable instance segmentation and species classification. Local benchmarks that jointly evaluate both steps, and that quantify how segmentation error propagates into taxonomic labels, remain scarce for temperate mixed forests. This thesis builds manually refined TLS instance references for two University of Freiburg research forests in southwestern Germany: a beech-dominated Ettenheim plot (approximately \(50\,\mathrm{m}\times 50\,\mathrm{m}\); 128 ground-truth trees) and a denser spruce-dominated Mathislewald subplot (approximately \(25\,\mathrm{m}\times 25\,\mathrm{m}\); 72 ground-truth trees). ForestMamba and SegmentAnyTree are compared against that geometric reference using point-count IoU matching. Inventory stems are linked exclusively to manual trees, and DetailView is applied to the same matched stems under manual, SegmentAnyTree, ForestMamba and ForestMamba+BluePoint instance tracks. At IoU threshold \(0.5\), under this manually refined reference ForestMamba achieved higher plot-level F1 on both sites (\(70.3\%\) at Ettenheim; \(86.3\%\) at Mathislewald), whereas SegmentAnyTree was competitive at Ettenheim (F1 \(64.2\%\)) but much weaker at Mathislewald in the final absolute-coordinate comparison (F1 \(21.5\%\)). Those rankings are conditional on a reference that was edited from preliminary ForestMamba instances. Per-tree hard F1 related to height, nearest-neighbour packing and convex-hull volume in exploratory associations (trees within subplots are not independent replicates): packing dominated at Ettenheim, while height and hull occupancy dominated at Mathislewald. On inventory-matched stems, DetailView overall accuracy on manual instances was \(49.5\%\) at Ettenheim (\(N=95\)) and \(62.5\%\) at Mathislewald (\(N=32\)); the Mathislewald figure is sample-limited (a few label flips move OA by several points) and is limited mainly by open-set taxonomic transfer and class imbalance rather than by delineation quality. Free space did not significantly improve species accuracy, and large hull volume at Ettenheim tracked poorer labels through majority beech failure. Switching to automatic instances changed stem-level overall accuracy by only a few percentage points at Ettenheim and by noisier, sample-limited amounts at Mathislewald; this ablation measures classification given matched instances, not end-to-end species-map accuracy. The study contributes a reproducible local reference protocol and evidence that improving instances or packing-based triage alone is unlikely to close the taxonomic gap without local adaptation.

Introduction

Understanding which trees grow where, and in what mixture, size, and condition, is foundational to forest science. Composition and spatial distribution are not peripheral descriptors; they are the substrate on which almost every subsequent inference rests. Allometric biomass estimates, habitat assessments, growth and yield projections, and evaluations of management interventions each inherit the quality of the underlying tree list. In that sense, a forest-analysis chain is only as strong as its weakest link. For decades, that weakest link has often been the acquisition of individual-tree information itself: field campaigns that are time-intensive, costly, constrained by access, and difficult to sustain at the spatial and temporal density that modern questions demand [1-4]1. Against that background, this thesis develops high-quality reference data for two university research forests in southwestern Germany and uses them to benchmark contemporary deep-learning workflows for individual-tree analysis. We compare two instance-segmentation approaches against manually refined terrestrial laser-scanning (TLS) tree delineations, and we evaluate a species-classification workflow on inventory-matched stems under both manual and automatically derived instances. The intent is not merely to report scores, but to quantify how delineation quality affects stem-level species labels on inventory-matched trees and to document a reproducible local protocol that others can extend.

Research questions

  1. How accurately do current deep-learning instance-segmentation models recover manually verified individual trees in complex temperate TLS scenes, and how do candidate models differ in detection versus delineation behaviour?
  2. How accurately does a state-of-the-art species-classification workflow assign taxonomic labels to inventory-matched stems when instances are taken from manual reference delineations?
  3. To what extent do automatic segmentation errors propagate into stem-level species classification accuracy on the same inventory-matched trees relative to the manual-instance baseline?

Objectives

Correspondingly, we aim to (i) curate manually refined TLS instance references co-registered with university field inventory where applicable; (ii) benchmark two segmentation models against that geometric reference using standardized overlap and detection metrics; (iii) benchmark species classification on inventory ground truth across manual and automated instance tracks on matched stems; and (iv) synthesize strengths, limitations, and implications for transferable, scientifically rigorous automated forest inventory.

Background

Study sites

This thesis is based on two temperate research forests in Baden-Württemberg, southwestern Germany. At each site field inventory and terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) were collected over the same stand footprint.

Ettenheim (ECOSENSE)

The Ettenheim site lies in the western foothills of the Black Forest near the town of Ettenheim, on the eastern margin of the Upper Rhine Plain (approximately 48.2680°N, 7.8782°E). Elevation is comparatively low for the wider Black Forest region, and the local climate is milder and warmer than that of the highlands to the southeast, with a temperate, seasonally distinct regime typical of the Upper Rhine / western Black Forest fringe.

The research plot analysed here measures approximately \(50\,\mathrm{m}\times 50\,\mathrm{m}\) (\(2500\,\mathrm{m}^{2}\)). Within this footprint the field inventory records 115 stems. Composition is strongly beech-dominated: European beech (Fagus sylvatica) accounts for 71.3% of inventoried stems, followed by silver fir (Abies alba, 15.7%) and Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii, 11.3%), with Norway spruce (Picea abies) and European larch (Larix decidua) each below 1%.

Mathislewald (Hinterzarten)

The Mathislewald site lies in the southern Black Forest near Hinterzarten, adjacent to Mathisleweiher (approximately 47.8847°N, 8.0885°E). It sits substantially higher than Ettenheim and experiences a cooler, more humid montane climate typical of the Black Forest uplands, with cooler summers, colder winters, and higher precipitation than the Upper Rhine fringe.

The research plot analysed here measures approximately \(25\,\mathrm{m}\times 25\,\mathrm{m}\) (\(625\,\mathrm{m}^{2}\)). Within this footprint the field inventory records 34 stems. Composition is spruce-dominated: Norway spruce (Picea abies) accounts for 64.7% of inventoried stems, followed by European beech (Fagus sylvatica, 23.5%) and silver fir (Abies alba, 11.8%).

Methodology

The Background and Data sections establish that field inventory and terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) were already available for Ettenheim and Mathislewald. The methodological chain below therefore starts from those source products and documents how the analysis footprint was narrowed, how reference instances were built, how segmentation models were compared to that reference, how inventory stems were linked to manually delineated trees and how species classification was evaluated on the resulting matched set. All geospatial processing used the projected coordinate reference system EPSG:32632.

Inventory-guided spatial narrowing of the TLS clouds

The delivered TLS scenes extend beyond the inventoried stems. To restrict subsequent work to the area that is jointly supported by inventory and proximal geometry, inventory stem positions were treated as a planar point set and enclosed with a concave hull. A fixed buffer of \(10\,\mathrm{m}\) was then applied around that hull, and the buffered polygon was used to clip the TLS point cloud.

At Ettenheim the concave hull was computed with Shapely's concave-hull routine using a ratio of \(0.05\) and without holes. At Mathislewald an adaptive ratio schedule was used \((0.2\), \(0.15\), \(0.25\), \(0.3\), \(0.1\), \(0.35\), \(0.4\), \(0.5\), \(1.0)\) until the hull perimeter contained at most \(120\) vertices; if no ratio satisfied that constraint the convex hull was used instead. In both cases the clip polygon was required to cover every valid inventory point. Clipping was performed in streaming fashion with laspy so that only points whose planimetric coordinates fell inside the buffered polygon were retained.

Bird's-eye verification plots were produced for both sites by overlaying a thinned view of the source point cloud, the inventory stem locations, the inner concave hull outline and the buffered clip boundary (Figure 3). These maps confirmed that the retained cloud surrounds the inventoried stand with a controlled margin rather than truncating stems at the inventory extent.

Subdivision into labelled subplots

The inventory-clipped clouds remain large relative to interactive three-dimensional editing. Each clipped scene was therefore partitioned into square subplots of \(25\,\mathrm{m}\times 25\,\mathrm{m}\) (\(625\,\mathrm{m}^{2}\)). The subplot grid was centred on the clip-polygon centroid. Only cells that intersected the clip polygon and retained more than \(1\,\mathrm{m}^{2}\) of clipped area were kept. Subplots were numbered in a ring-clockwise order starting from the centre (tile_001, tile_002, tile_003, ...).

Within each subplot, preliminary ForestMamba instance labels already present in the source clouds were used to export one LAZ file per predicted tree, together with ground and residual catch-all layers. Trees with any point within \(0.12\,\mathrm{m}\) of the outer clip boundary were excluded from the valid tree set so that incomplete edge crowns would not enter later reference construction. The resulting subplot layouts were mapped and labelled for both sites (Figure 4).

Manual reference construction and all subsequent benchmarks reported in this thesis focus on the subplots that were completed as CloudCompare projects: Ettenheim tile_001-tile_004 (approximately \(50\,\mathrm{m}\times 50\,\mathrm{m}\) of analysed footprint) and Mathislewald tile_001 (approximately \(25\,\mathrm{m}\times 25\,\mathrm{m}\)). The remaining subplots of the clipped scenes were retained as an organised archive but were not manually annotated. Instance-segmentation evaluation protocol

Automatic instance predictions were compared with the manual CloudCompare reference under a shared point support. All points belonging to the analysed subplot projects (tree layers, catch-alls and ground) defined the evaluation set \(X_{\mathrm{eval}}\). The same XYZ locations were then extracted from the ForestMamba and SegmentAnyTree products so that every point carried a manual ground-truth instance identifier and a predicted instance identifier. Ground, catch-all and non-tree material received ground-truth identifier -1. Point-count Intersection-over-Union (IoU) was computed between each predicted instance and each ground-truth tree. Predicted instances with fewer than \(100\) points were discarded in the primary protocol (a common noise filter for tiny fragments). As a sensitivity check we repeated plot-level matching at \(n_{\mathrm{min}}\in\{0,25,50,100,200\}\). Remaining predictions were assigned to ground-truth trees by greedy one-to-one maximisation of IoU. A match was accepted only when IoU was at least \(\tau=0.5\). As a further sensitivity check we also solved the same IoU matrix with the Hungarian algorithm (global bipartite assignment maximising total IoU, still rejecting pairs below \(\tau\)) and compared plot-level precision, recall, F1 and coverage at \(\tau=0.5\). The primary reported tables and figures retain greedy matching with \(n_{\mathrm{min}}=100\). Label transfer onto \(X_{\mathrm{eval}}\) used exact millimetre XYZ hashing where possible and nearest-neighbour fill within \(0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) otherwise.

From the matched assignments we computed precision, recall, F1-score and coverage (mean over ground-truth trees of the best IoU, without the \(\tau\) gate), together with true-positive, false-positive and false-negative counts. Secondary diagnostics recorded over-segmentation and under-segmentation patterns using multi-overlap inspection at IoU \(\ge 0.25\). The same matching was repeated for a threshold sweep \(\tau\in\{0.3,0.4,0.5,0.6,0.7\}\) and for per-subplot strata. Per-tree pairwise F1 was additionally related to simple structural covariates derived from the manual clouds (height as the difference between the 99th and 5th percentiles of \(z\), planimetric nearest-neighbour distance and a convex-hull volume proxy) so that later interpretation can separate detection failures from delineation failures. Associations used two-sided Spearman rank correlations on per-tree scores. Trees within a subplot are spatially dependent, so \(N\) trees are not \(N\) independent experimental replicates (Ettenheim: four subplots; Mathislewald: one). We therefore treat that battery as exploratory: uncorrected \(p<0.05\) is used for narrative emphasis and we still report \(\rho\) and \(p\) for non-significant associations, while Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate \(q\)-values within each site provide a multiplicity-aware sensitivity summary. Effect sizes (tercile means and \(\rho\)) are emphasised over binary significance. ForestFormer3D itself was not run as a third predictor; it provided only the evaluation template.

Visual co-registration of inventory and manual trees

Before formal inventory assignment, inventory stem positions were inspected against the manually segmented trees in plan view. A local ground surface was fitted to the manually labelled ground points of each subplot and a thin breast-height slice was extracted at \(1.3\,\mathrm{m}\) above ground along the local surface normal, with a tolerance of \(\pm 0.05\,\mathrm{m}\). Inventory stems were drawn as circles whose radii reflected recorded diameters at breast height.

These overlays were used to check whether residual geospatial offset between inventory coordinates and the TLS scene would systematically prevent matching. Where a stem was visibly offset from its inventory marker but clearly represented the same tree on the basis of position, height and diameter context, the association was retained for later assignment rather than discarded as a geolocation conflict. At Mathislewald a translational shift of the inventory overlay was estimated from paired stem markers before final matching; at Ettenheim interactive overlay adjustment was available for the same visual check. Figure 5 shows the breast-height overlays used for this inspection at both sites.

Exclusive assignment of inventory stems to trees

Inventory stems and manual tree layers were then linked by an exclusive one-to-one rule on the same breast-height geometry. For each inventory stem and each manual layer the planimetric distance between stem centre and layer breast-height centroid was required to satisfy equation dist r_inv + r_layer + 0.75 m, equation where \(r_{\mathrm{inv}}\) is the inventory radius at breast height and \(r_{\mathrm{layer}}\) is the radius of the layer's breast-height point envelope. Among geometrically eligible candidates, assignment was exclusive: each inventory stem and each layer could enter at most one pair.

When several layers lay within \(0.75\,\mathrm{m}\) of the nearest eligible distance to the same inventory stem, ties were broken with an attribute cost based on the logarithmic diameter ratio and the relative height difference between inventory and layer. Incomplete stubs shorter than \(3\,\mathrm{m}\) were strongly penalised when competing for a real inventory stem, and layers with more points were mildly preferred when other cues were similar. A small number of visually confirmed but geometrically awkward pairs were entered through an explicit override list after inspection, while layers known to be unsuitable for species evaluation were excluded from the matched set.

Unmatched inventory stems and unmatched manual layers were reviewed separately. Unmatched inventory positions were visualised as vertical marker poles in the three-dimensional scene to support inspection of possible missed trees, incomplete delineations or inventory points outside the annotated subplot. Unmatched manual layers were inspected for over-split fragments, edge trees and non-tree residuals. Only exclusive inventory-matched manual trees were retained for species classification. Species-classification performance is therefore conditional on successful inventory-to-instance registration (including visual overrides where used) and should not be interpreted as plot-wide classification accuracy on every manual or automatic tree. Stable instance identifiers were then written for those stems: at Ettenheim \(\mathrm{PredInstance}=\mathrm{plot}\times 1000+\mathrm{tree}\), and at Mathislewald \(\mathrm{PredInstance}\) followed the inventory tree identifier.

Analysis products and intended inference

The pipeline yields three families of analysis products. First, subplot-wise and pooled instance-segmentation tables and figures quantify detection and delineation of ForestMamba and SegmentAnyTree against the manual CloudCompare reference, including behaviour across IoU thresholds and structural covariates. Second, exclusive match manifests linking inventory stems to trees document which stems enter the taxonomic evaluation and which inventory or layer residuals remain unmatched after geometric and visual review. Third, DetailView stem-level tables and figures quantify taxonomic agreement with inventory labels under manual and automatic instance sources and isolate the change attributable to switching the instance track. Inventory-matched stems were further joined to the height, nearest-neighbour and hull-volume covariates of their matched manual layers so that species accuracy could be stratified by the same structural axes used for instance F1, again using exploratory two-sided Spearman tests with Benjamini-Hochberg \(q\)-values within site as a multiplicity-aware sensitivity summary.

These products are intended to answer, without circular use of inventory as geometric ground truth, (i) how well current instance-segmentation models recover manually verified trees in these temperate TLS scenes under this reference, (ii) how accurately DetailView recovers inventory taxonomy when instances are taken from the manual reference on matched stems and (iii) how much changing the instance source on those same stems changes stem-level species classification relative to the manual-instance baseline. Numerical outcomes are reported in the Results section.

Discussion

Instance segmentation (RQ1)

Reference extent and inventory matching

The Ettenheim reference comprises four completed \(25\,\mathrm{m}\) subplots (approximately \(50\,\mathrm{m}\times 50\,\mathrm{m}\)) with 128 manual ground-truth trees on \(26\,997\,057\) evaluation points (\(22\,714\,670\) tree points). Per-subplot counts of 25, 41, 38 and 24 indicate uneven stocking, with tiles 002 and 003 carrying the largest tree loads. Mathislewald contributes one annotated subplot (approximately \(25\,\mathrm{m}\times 25\,\mathrm{m}\)) with 72 ground-truth trees on \(24\,260\,295\) evaluation points (\(14\,605\,364\) tree points). Tree count per unit area and tree-point density are both higher than on any Ettenheim tile, consistent with a more crowded proximal scene.

These \(N_{\mathrm{GT}}\) values define the denominators for recall and related detection metrics and are distinct from the inventory-matched stems used for species classification. Exclusive assignment matched 95 of 115 inventory stems at Ettenheim (20 unmatched) and 32 of 34 at Mathislewald (2 unmatched), while 12 and 157 manual layers remained unmatched. At Ettenheim the census is nearly complete: most inventoried stems map to a unique manual layer, with a modest residual for review. At Mathislewald almost every inventory stem is matched, but the large unmatched-layer residual is attributable to over-split fragments, stubs and non-inventory material relative to a short inventory of 34 stems rather than to 157 omitted field trees. Inventory matching therefore selects the taxonomic evaluation set; it does not redefine the geometric reference.

Qualitative instance colourings place ForestMamba and SegmentAnyTree on the same point support as the manual reference, so metric differences reflect label assignment rather than changes in geometry. Breast-height inventory overlays show markers both coincident with and offset from layer centroids; residual offsets motivate the visual co-registration step and contribute to assignment uncertainty noted under Limitations.

Plot-level performance

At \(\tau=0.5\), ForestMamba achieved high recall on both sites (91.4% Ettenheim; 91.7% Mathislewald), exceeding SegmentAnyTree (75.8%; 23.6%). Coverage ranked identically. Precision diverged by site: ForestMamba over-predicted at Ettenheim (\(N_{\mathrm{pred}}=205\) versus 128 ground-truth trees; precision 57.1%) but approached the ground-truth count at Mathislewald (precision 81.5%). SegmentAnyTree retained moderate precision at Ettenheim (55.7%) and performed poorly at Mathislewald (19.8%), with 69 false positives and 55 false negatives among 86 predictions. Plot-level F1 favoured ForestMamba on both sites under this reference (70.3% versus 64.2% at Ettenheim; 86.3% versus 21.5% at Mathislewald). The between-model gap is modest in the mixed beech stand and large in the denser spruce subplot.

ForestMamba therefore operates as a high-recall detector whose precision cost is site-dependent, whereas SegmentAnyTree is competitive at Ettenheim but highly sensitive to local packing and product alignment at Mathislewald. Final SegmentAnyTree labels used absolute-coordinate partner products rather than earlier height-normalized transfers; the Mathislewald result accordingly reflects both model behaviour and product preparation under the shared evaluation protocol.

Ettenheim subplot heterogeneity

ForestMamba F1 ranged from 72.7% (tile 001) to 63.0% (tile 004), with coverage remaining high (80.3-94.3%). SegmentAnyTree F1 ranged from 65.4% (tile 002) to 46.5% (tile 003), where coverage fell to 52.9% and 15 trees were missed. ForestMamba false negatives remained low across tiles (1-5). Error centroids are distributed through subplot interiors rather than confined to edges. Plot-level aggregates therefore mask tile-level failure modes that would matter under operational acceptance thresholds.

IoU threshold dependence

Across \(\tau\in\{0.3,\ldots,0.7\}\), ForestMamba F1 at Ettenheim declined only from 70.9% to 66.1%, indicating that accepted matches largely exceed the default gate. SegmentAnyTree declined from 68.9% to 50.3%, indicating a larger share of marginal overlaps. At Mathislewald, ForestMamba declined from 90.2% to 68.0% while remaining usable at high \(\tau\); SegmentAnyTree declined from 43.0% to 10.1%. Per-tree maximum-IoU distributions agree: ForestMamba concentrates at higher best IoU on both sites, whereas SegmentAnyTree at Mathislewald concentrates at low best IoU, so increasing \(\tau\) removes most of its true positives.

Error taxonomy and spatial structure

Over- and under-segmentation counts at \(\tau=0.5\) are small relative to misses and leakage-flagged true positives. At Ettenheim, ForestMamba recorded 11 misses and 10 leakage-flagged matches; SegmentAnyTree recorded 31 and 42. At Mathislewald, ForestMamba recorded 6 misses and 14 leakage-flagged matches; SegmentAnyTree recorded 55 misses. Leakage-flagged matches pass the IoU gate while sharing substantial point mass with neighbouring trees or background and therefore indicate delineation defects among detections. SegmentAnyTree's elevated leakage-flag rate at Ettenheim, despite usable F1, shows that many accepted objects remain geometrically impure. Lower ForestMamba leakage-flag counts indicate an advantage in object purity when products are well aligned, beyond detection alone.

Plot F1 versus macro hard F1

Macro hard F1 weights each ground-truth tree equally; plot F1 is dominated by the global TP/FP/FN pool. At Ettenheim, ForestMamba macro hard F1 (88.3%; 95% CI 83.2-92.9) exceeds plot F1 (70.3%), consistent with strong delineation among detected trees and a large false-positive penalty at plot scale. SegmentAnyTree values are closer (67.5% versus 64.2%). At Mathislewald, ForestMamba macro and plot scores are similar (82.0% versus 86.3%); SegmentAnyTree remains low on both (19.7% versus 21.5%). The Ettenheim ForestMamba contrast shows that a detection-heavy error mode can coexist with high per-tree overlap once a match exists.

Structural covariates

Structural covariates separate two failure regimes under exploratory two-sided Spearman tests (uncorrected \(p<0.05\) for narrative emphasis; Benjamini-Hochberg \(q\) within site in the correlation tables). Non-significant \(p\)-values are retained below. At Ettenheim, local packing is the strongest continuous associate of per-tree hard F1: Spearman \(\rho\) with nearest-neighbour distance is \(0.28\) (\(p=0.0016\), \(q=0.0047\)) for ForestMamba and \(0.41\) (\(p<0.001\), \(q<0.001\)) for SegmentAnyTree, and both remain positive after residualising on point count. Crowded stems (low NN) lose roughly 22 percentage points of ForestMamba hard F1 and 45 points of SegmentAnyTree hard F1 relative to the most open tercile. That pattern matches the expectation that interlocking crowns and short inter-stem gaps increase boundary leakage and missed detections. Height and hull volume move in the same direction as size, but ForestMamba height (\(\rho=0.04\), \(p=0.68\)) and both models' hull-volume correlations (ForestMamba \(\rho=0.08\), \(p=0.36\); SegmentAnyTree \(\rho=0.17\), \(p=0.052\)) do not meet \(p<0.05\); ForestMamba in particular already scores highly across much of the height range, so a height cutoff alone would discard little of its residual error. Readers who prefer a slightly laxer threshold may note that SegmentAnyTree hull volume sits just above \(0.05\).

At Mathislewald the ranking reverses. Height and hull volume dominate (SegmentAnyTree \(\rho=0.56\), \(p<0.001\) and \(\rho=0.54\), \(p<0.001\); ForestMamba \(\rho=0.34\), \(p=0.0036\) and \(\rho=0.27\), \(p=0.020\)), whereas nearest-neighbour distance is non-significant (ForestMamba \(\rho=0.16\), \(p=0.19\); SegmentAnyTree \(\rho=0.02\), \(p=0.87\)). The subplot is already tightly packed over a narrow NN range, so residual free space among centroids carries little additional information. What remains is a size and occupancy gradient: small, low-volume objects are almost never recovered by SegmentAnyTree (lowest height and hull terciles near 3-6% hard F1) while larger stems partially recover. ForestMamba retains a higher floor on small objects but still improves toward the upper terciles. Failures are therefore not confined to a single structural niche; Ettenheim errors concentrate where neighbours are close, whereas Mathislewald SegmentAnyTree errors concentrate on small occupancy even when a neighbour gap exists.

Overall, current deep-learning instance segmentation recovers most manually verified trees in these TLS scenes when products are well prepared. Under the shared protocol ForestMamba scores higher on both sites on this reference. Because editing began from preliminary ForestMamba instances, that ranking is conditional on the reference construction and should not be read as an unconditional claim of superiority over SegmentAnyTree on independently delineated ground truth. The models differ in detection versus delineation behaviour: ForestMamba is high-recall and precision-limited at Ettenheim; SegmentAnyTree is more threshold-sensitive and can fail under adverse packing or small-object occupancy. Large-occupancy matched stems at Ettenheim are dominated by European beech, the class with the weakest diagonal in the confusion matrix; the hull-volume gradient therefore largely rediscovers the beech failure rather than a pure geometric effect of crown bulk. Automatic instance tracks reproduce the same decline, which indicates that the pattern is taxonomic rather than an artefact of one delineation source.

At Mathislewald the free-space hypothesis is not supported: crowded terciles have higher OA than open ones (81.8% versus 50.0% manual; \(\rho=-0.18\), \(p=0.32\)), and hull volume is flat (manual \(\rho=0.035\), \(p=0.85\)). With \(N=32\) and roughly ten stems per bin, that reversal should not be over-interpreted, but it warns against transferring Ettenheim packing rules to denser spruce subplots. Structural covariates useful for segmentation (packing at Ettenheim; size at Mathislewald) are therefore not interchangeable predictors of DetailView correctness.

Under manual instances, DetailView recovers inventory-compatible labels only moderately well. Accuracy is site- and class-dependent and is limited by open-set transfer into a small local taxonomy. Geometric instance quality and local free space are not the primary constraints on this baseline; class composition and transfer of the European head dominate.

Propagation of segmentation error into stem-level species labels (RQ3)

The same inventory-matched stems were classified under manual, SegmentAnyTree, ForestMamba and ForestMamba+BluePoint instances, isolating instance source as the experimental factor. Overall accuracy therefore conditions on that matched-stem set: trees missed by automatic segmentation outside the set, and unmatched manual layers, do not enter the score. The ablation isolates label propagation given instances rather than end-to-end species-map performance.

At Ettenheim, overall accuracy declined by 2.1 percentage points under SegmentAnyTree and by 4.2 under ForestMamba and ForestMamba+BluePoint relative to the manual track. Paired bootstrap 95% intervals for those \(\Delta\mathrm{OA}\) values all include zero, so the Ettenheim gaps are compatible with sampling noise on the matched stems. At Mathislewald, SegmentAnyTree exceeded the manual track by 6.3 percentage points, while ForestMamba and ForestMamba+BluePoint fell short by 6.3 and 12.5 points; paired intervals are wide (\(N=32\)) and do not support a strong causal ranking of instance tracks. BluePoint did not improve species OA relative to ForestMamba (\(0.0\) points at Ettenheim; \(-6.3\) at Mathislewald) and reduced macro F1 on both sites.

Stem-level label agreement between tracks was high for manual versus ForestMamba (80.0% Ettenheim; 81.3% Mathislewald) and manual versus SegmentAnyTree (74.7%; 81.3%), lower for manual versus ForestMamba+BluePoint (69.5%; 65.6%), and intermediate for ForestMamba versus ForestMamba+BluePoint (74.7%; 78.1%). Combined with modest OA gaps, high agreement indicates that many stems retain the same label (correct or incorrect) across instance sources. Segmentation error reassigns a minority of decisions rather than rewriting the taxonomic map.

Multi-track confusion matrices preserve true-class supports and redistribute residual mass among open-set and neighbouring taxa. At Ettenheim, Douglas fir remains comparatively stable across tracks, while beech and silver fir absorb more automatic-track degradation; per-class F1 declines accordingly under ForestMamba and ForestMamba+BluePoint. At Mathislewald, Norway spruce F1 remains high under SegmentAnyTree and weakens under ForestMamba+BluePoint; beech and silver fir fluctuate on small supports. Class-level shifts can therefore occur even when plot-level OA appears nearly flat.

Segmentation error propagates into stem-level species labels but is not the dominant barrier in this study. Ettenheim manual-to-automatic OA gaps are a few percentage points against a manual baseline near 50%. Scope and generalizability

The study is a local benchmark on two university research forests in southwestern Germany: a larger beech-dominated mixed stand at Ettenheim and a smaller spruce-dominated subplot at Mathislewald. Extrapolation to boreal plantations, tropical forests, leaf-off deciduous scenes or airborne-only inventories requires new references. Within temperate mixed TLS settings, the results are most informative for relative model behaviour under a shared protocol, for the magnitude of segmentation-to-species propagation on inventory-matched stems, and for the cost of building usable geometric and taxonomic references. Two ecologically distinct sites strengthen external validity; small matched \(N\) at Mathislewald, partial subplot coverage and the lack of an independent re-annotation weaken it.

Implications

For detection-oriented use, ForestMamba is viable at plot scale on these scenes if false positives are acceptable or filterable. For delineation-sensitive products (crown competition, biomass allometry, clean stem extraction), leakage-flag and \(\tau\)-sweep diagnostics are as informative as headline F1. Nearest-neighbour packing is a useful Ettenheim QA cue for geometric quality; at Mathislewald, object size and hull occupancy are more informative than residual centroid spacing. SegmentAnyTree remains a relevant Ettenheim baseline but requires careful absolute-coordinate product preparation.

Off-the-shelf DetailView is not a drop-in replacement for field taxonomy on these forests. Constrained local label sets, fine-tuning or human oversight remain necessary before automated species layers support management or ecological inference. Because instance-source effects on stem-level labels are modest relative to the manual-instance taxonomic gap, and because free-space and hull-volume filters do not recover beech performance at Ettenheim, investment in segmentation or packing-based stem triage alone will not close that taxonomic gap on matched stems. The paired manual-plus-inventory reference and dual instance/species protocol remain a reusable template for research forests that already hold TLS and stem maps.

Conclusions

We constructed manually refined TLS instance references for Ettenheim and Mathislewald, linked inventory species where exclusive matching succeeded, and benchmarked ForestMamba and SegmentAnyTree for detection and overlap together with DetailView for species classification on manual and automated instances.

For individual-tree recovery (RQ1), under the shared protocol and this manually refined reference ForestMamba achieved higher plot-level scores on both sites: high recall with usable F1 at Ettenheim and stronger balanced scores at Mathislewald. SegmentAnyTree remained competitive at Ettenheim but was much weaker at Mathislewald in the final absolute-coordinate comparison. Those rankings are conditional on a ForestMamba-seeded reference. Detection versus delineation diagnostics (threshold sweeps, leakage flags, macro hard F1) show that headline F1 alone understates how models differ.

For species labels on manual instances (RQ2), off-the-shelf DetailView reached only about half overall accuracy at Ettenheim and about three-fifths at Mathislewald on inventory-matched stems. Open-set transfer and local class imbalance dominate that gap. For propagation of instance-source effects into stem-level labels (RQ3), changing the instance track on the same matched stems moved overall accuracy by only a few points at Ettenheim and by noisier but still secondary amounts at the smaller Mathislewald matched set. That ablation does not score end-to-end species maps (missed or extra trees outside the matched set are not in the OA). Better segmentation helps only modestly on these stems; it does not by itself close the taxonomic gap.

The paired geometric and inventory reference, and the dual instance/species evaluation chain, provide a reusable local benchmark template. Priority next steps are independent re-annotation controls, larger matched samples, local species-head adaptation and cross-platform extensions using the broader ECOSENSE and WaldAgil sensing archives.
